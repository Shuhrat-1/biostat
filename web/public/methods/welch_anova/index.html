Welch's ANOVA (unequal variances)

Do the means of three or more groups differ when the spread within the groups is different? Welch's ANOVA compares the means without pooling the group variances into one.

Idea

Ordinary ANOVA estimates one variance for all groups — MSWMS_W. If the spread is large in one group and small in another, the pooled estimate fits neither: with unequal n, P comes out wrong [proposed: Glass et al. 1972].

Welch weights each group mean by its own precision: the weight wi=ni/si2w_i = n_i/s_i^2 is larger the smaller the spread and the more observations there are. FWF_W measures how far the means scatter around the weighted grand mean, and the denominator degrees of freedom are estimated from the data and are usually fractional [proposed: Welch 1951].

Bring the means almost together: the ordinary F loses the difference before Welch does — the common MS within is inflated by the spread of group b, while Welch gives b a small weight. On the example

Illustrative data set (map scenario S08): three groups of 10 values, built as 10, 20 and 30 plus normal noise with SD 1, 5 and 1 — the spread does not grow with the mean. Sample SDs: a 0.99, b 4.94, c 0.99. The variances are heterogeneous (Bartlett X² = 28.9 > 5.99), and the map gives B4.5. FWF_W = 982.5 on (2; 16.2) df, P = 1.2·10⁻¹⁷; ordinary F = 113.7 on (2; 27) df, P = 7.1·10⁻¹⁴. Games–Howell: 3 of 3 pairs differ. Signal B4.1 (pooled residuals non-normal) fires too: a mixture of residuals with different SDs is not normal, although the values within each group were built as normal.

[proposed: Welch 1951]; Welham et al. (2015), p. 107 (analysis with unequal variances not linked to the mean); Witte & Witte (2017), p. 316.

Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths

  • Does not need equal variances. P is correct with unequal σ² and unequal n — where the ordinary F goes wrong [proposed: Welch 1951; Glass et al. 1972].
  • Computed from group summaries: only n, the means and the variances are needed (Witte, p. 316).
  • For two groups it is Welch's t-test: FW=tW2F_W = t_W^2 [proposed: Welch 1951].

Weaknesses

  • No common MSWMS_W — no single SED and LSD: each pair in the comparisons has its own SE (Games–Howell).
  • Requires normality within groups, like ordinary ANOVA (Welham, p. 107).
  • With small n the weights are unstable: the si2s_i^2 in a weight is a rough estimate, and a group with a small SD by chance gets too large a weight. With equal variances and n = 5, the ratio of sample SDs exceeds 2 in 22 % of cases [BioStat: simulation, audit 01].
  • A group without spread stops the calculation: if all values in a group are equal, its weight is undefined — BioStat reports an error [BioStat].
  • If the SDs grow with the means, transforming the response is better: the variances even out, and the effects become ratios (Welham, p. 96–98, 114–118).

Welham et al. (2015), p. 96–98, 107, 114–118; Witte & Witte (2017), p. 316; [proposed: Welch 1951; Glass et al. 1972; Games & Howell 1976]; [BioStat].

How the map chooses this method

The map leads here with signal B4.5: the group variances are heterogeneous (Bartlett's test) and at the same time do not grow with the means. If the SDs grow with the means, the same spread is explained by the scale, B4.5 stays silent, and the map leads to a transformation (B4.3).

Heterogeneity is decided by the same rule as the warning of one-way ANOVA (the shared function variance_rule). With an outlier candidate, B4.5 becomes a hint: check the record first.

  • B4.3 A link between SD and the means — leads to a transformation instead of Welch.
  • B4.4 (hint) Strong skewness of residuals (|g₁| > 1) — a reason to transform.
  • DIAG.3 Fewer than 10 residual df — a warning about low power.

What next

A significant FWF_W says only that the means are not all equal. The method itself shows which pairs differ — Games–Howell comparisons: each pair has its own SE and Welch–Satterthwaite df, P and 95 % CI of the difference [proposed: Games & Howell 1976]. The comparisons after ANOVA (B5) use the common MSWMS_W and are unsuitable with unequal variances. If the goal is to show that there are no differences, that is a different task: the equivalence test (TOST); a non-significant F does not prove the absence of differences (Welham, p. 253).
