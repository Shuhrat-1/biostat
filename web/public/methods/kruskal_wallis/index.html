Kruskal-Wallis test (nonparametric)

Do three or more groups differ when the response is a score or its distribution is far from normal? The Kruskal–Wallis test replaces the values with ranks and compares the rank sums of the groups.

Idea

All observations are ranked together: the smallest gets rank 1, the largest rank N, and tied values get the mean of their ranks (Witte, p. 390, 396–397). If the groups do not differ, their mean ranks are close; H grows as they move apart.

Means are not what is compared: a rejected H0H_0 only means “the group distributions differ” (Witte, p. 396–399). One can speak of a difference in medians if the distributions in the groups have the same shape and differ only by a shift [proposed: Hollander & Wolfe 1999].

The hypothesis tested:

H0: the response distributions in all t groups are the sameH_0:\ \text{the response distributions in all } t \text{ groups are the same}

The H statistic

H=12N(N+1)∑iRi2ni−3(N+1)=13.88H = \frac{12}{N(N+1)} \sum_i \frac{R_i^2}{n_i} - 3(N+1) = 13.88

The book's formula (Witte, p. 396–397): RiR_i is the rank sum of group i, nin_i its size.

Correction for ties and P

C=1−∑(τ3−τ)N3−N=0.949,HC=14.62,df=t−1=2,P<0.001C = 1 - \frac{\sum(\tau^3-\tau)}{N^3-N} = 0.949,\qquad \frac{H}{C} = 14.62,\qquad df = t-1 = 2,\qquad P < 0.001

BioStat always divides H by C [proposed: Kruskal & Wallis 1952]; Witte does not introduce the correction and advises thinking about ties only if the test is barely significant (p. 400). P comes from χ² with t−1t-1 df: the approximation is accurate enough when each group has at least 4 observations (Witte, p. 398).

On the example

Illustrative data set (map scenario S12): scores 1–5, three groups of 8. Rank sums: a 45, b 105, c 150; medians 2, 3 and 4.5. With only five scores there are many ties, so the correction is noticeable: H = 13.88 without it and 14.62 with it on 2 df, P < 0.001. The map gives the hint B4.8; the hint R2.2 (non-negative integers look like counts) fires on the same numbers, and the choice between them depends on what the response means.

Witte & Witte (2017), p. 386–387, 390, 396–400; Welham et al. (2015), p. 127; correction C — [proposed: Kruskal & Wallis 1952]; interpretation via medians — [proposed: Hollander & Wolfe 1999].

Strengths and weaknesses

Strengths

  • Does not need normality — works with ranks (Witte, p. 386–387).
  • Suits scores and ranks, where the mean is poorly defined (Witte, p. 386; Welham, p. 127).
  • An extreme value is just an extreme rank: an outlier affects H less than F [proposed: Hollander & Wolfe 1999].
  • The report has the rank sums, the uncorrected H and the group medians, as in the book (Witte, p. 396–399) [BioStat].

Weaknesses

  • Less powerful than ANOVA when the ANOVA assumptions hold (Witte, p. 387).
  • Answers about distributions, not means: no SED, LSD or CIs of differences of means (Witte, p. 396–399).
  • P is approximate (χ²): accurate enough when each group has at least 4 observations (Witte, p. 398); with smaller groups BioStat adds a note — the approximation usually overstates P (with 3 groups of 3: 0.027 against the exact 0.0036) [BioStat: audit 02-02].
  • H does not say which groups differ. The standard pairwise comparisons after H are Dunn's test [proposed: Dunn 1964]; BioStat does not have it.
  • One factor without blocks. For scores in blocks (each treatment once in every block) — the Friedman test; for scores in an experiment with several factors — ordinal regression (Welham, p. 513).

Witte & Witte (2017), p. 386–387, 396–399; Welham et al. (2015), p. 127, 513; [BioStat]; [proposed: Dunn 1964].

The map leads here only with the hint B4.8: the response takes a few integer values in a narrow range (3 to 10 distinct values, range at most 10) — it looks like scores [BioStat]. With two groups the same hint leads to Mann–Whitney, and in a complete block experiment (role “block”) — to the Friedman test.

Non-normal residuals alone do not lead here: in three or more groups with homogeneous variances the map suggests the permutation test (B4.6), and with skewness — a transformation (B4.4).

What the map checks at this leaf

  • B4.8 (hint) The response looks like scores: 3 to 10 distinct integer values, range at most 10 [BioStat].
  • R2.2 (hint) Non-negative integers also look like counts: if the response is a number of objects rather than a score, a count model (GLM) is needed.
  • B4.1 Non-normality of residuals is checked but does not lead to this leaf: with three or more groups it is a note.

What next

A significant H says that the group distributions are not all the same, but not which ones differ. The standard pairwise comparisons after H are Dunn's test [proposed: Dunn 1964]; BioStat does not have it. The comparisons after ANOVA (B5) are based on means and the common MSWMS_W — they do not apply to a rank analysis.
